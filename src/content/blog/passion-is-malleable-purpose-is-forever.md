When someone picks a career path, they have to weigh a number of variables. Two of the most important are **impact** and **enjoyment**.

- **Impact** refers to the amount of **good** you bring about with your work.[^1]
- **Enjoyment** refers to how much utility you derive from your work. It can be fuzzily broken down into 2 components: day-to-day gratification and an ambient sense of purpose.

Let’s say you’re deciding between two jobs. Job 1 is **handily more impactful** but involves work you don’t find particularly enjoyable. Job 2 is less impactful but you’re confident you’ll enjoy your everyday tasks due to past experience. I think you should **always take Job 1**, here’s my reasoning...

*Note: I’m not really sure where compensation fits into this. It usually directly enhances both variables, hence I’m not factoring it into my definition of impact or enjoyment.*

### Impact at the cost of enjoyment

Anecdotally, I feel like people think that increasing the amount you do for others *inherently* decreases your own valence (through a new self-imposed burden). I think this stems from a society-wide intuition that associates “doing good” with small-scale physical gestures (i.e. helping an old lady cross the street). Put crudely, such acts are admirable because someone experiences a bad thing so others can experience good things.

This assumption is wrong. People generally feel **better** after doing good deeds, regardless of whether what they did was physically or mentally costly! In fact, I’d posit that people feel **better if they strain themselves more** when doing a good thing, assuming the outcome matches their effort.

### Pursue impact consistently

If you’re the type of person that feels better when you do one of the aforementioned good deeds, you’d probably be consistently happier if you help others more often. Since picking an impactful career is far and away the best way to operationalize this... (standard EA pitch ensues).

Seriously though, humans are blessed with the wonderful power of **aid**, a process by which someone can improve their own and another’s experience for the price of one action! On average, people who consistently help others are profoundly happier than those who don’t! Here’s a few reasons why:

1. Helpful activities often involve social interaction, generally with pleasant and well-meaning people.
2. The feeling of knowing you improved someone’s life is **addicting** and helps people find the “ambient sense of purpose” I referred to earlier.
3. Through interactions with those less fortunate, helpful activities often give you a sense of how lucky you are, increasing your gratitude and contentment.

### Your enjoyment is malleable

Now, I hear you say: *“This is all well and good, but I’ve wanted to play the oboe professionally ever since I was a lad.”*

To that, I’d concede that it could be possible you will only find consistent enjoyment from one type of activity. However, I’d stake a lot of money that you’re more multifaceted than you think. You have limited data on what you’re actually “passionate” about. There’s likely many areas of work you’d grow to love if given proper time. I’m not saying career transitions can’t be hard and confusing. I’m saying they’re almost certainly worth the effort if you can increase your impact!

Entrepreneurs generally start ventures because they’re interested in certain fields (e.g. gym founders generally like fitness). However, an entrepreneur’s work obligations almost immediately and permanently shift from their initial task of interest to a host of administrative duties like recruitment, project direction, and financial management. Despite this, founders generally grow to love their day-to-day more as their business progresses (assuming they find financial stability). Leading a venture lets entrepreneurs have greater impact, further a personal mission, and affect lives in ways they simply couldn't before; Demis Hassabis isn't sad he doesn't write code anymore.

Put simply, you can become passionate about almost any line of work if you have the requisite motivation, and what’s more motivating than the idea of profoundly improving peoples’ lives?

### So…

Think about which impactful careers your skills could be applied to. Prioritize how a job lets you help others. Whether a job is your “passion” should be a secondary consideration.

Passion follows from positive feedback. Making the world better is the ultimate source of positive feedback. Thus, you should chase impact, even from a purely selfish perspective!

[^1]: I’m a utilitarian. By good, I’m abstractly referring to counterfactual utility created. Thus, a very impactful job could be one that massively reduces suffering (e.g. working for [LEEP](https://leadelimination.org/)) or greatly increases pleasure (e.g. being Charli XCX).
