I originally wrote this to uncork my own bottlenecks, but I've put it here in case other bloggers need a slightly more articulate excuse to stop procrastinating.

A serious personal goal of mine is to write coherently, eloquently, and thoroughly about topics I find interesting. Currently, I do write with decent frequency — I'd estimate an hour of scribbling per night — yet I still yearn for more of a specific and rare kind of writing: that lucid, delectable flavor that arrives when you're hellbent on capturing and articulating an idea that's been scratching at your brain for a while. This note aims to make that kind of writing slightly easier to find.

### Mapping the mind's writing modes

When I'm not especially focused on my task at hand, I often enter some sort of autopilot-like state where my fingers type some vague and nondescript recomputation of whatever subject I'm nominally supposed to be engaging with. It's quite strange. When this happens, I'm often left with a weird, almost Claude-ish sentence fragment that runs just parallel to what I actually wanted to articulate. Switching off autopilot requires focus. Music and environmental distractions generally impinge on focus, while I've recently found that narrating my thoughts aloud can promote it.

Exploratory writing refers to writing done with the express purpose of exploring a topic for its own ends. I do this frequently, and should probably do it more. It brings its own risks, though: exploratory writing is nearly indulgent, as you're wading into an idea without a verifiable end state to strive towards or measure your progress against. This can lead you to dig, and subsequently go down, rabbit holes. This can be great! Random forays usually help patch holes in your world model, which is what exploratory writing aims to do. However, setting out without a specific destination means you're at risk of veering off course. Thus, ask yourself every so often: Am I actually exploring what I want to?

Directed writing refers to the kind of writing you do when you have an essay and an imminent deadline, or a well-scoped problem and a blank Google Doc on which to disambiguate and solve it. Directed writing, in my experience, is actually easier than exploratory writing because it demarcates non-obviously useful tangents as out of scope, meaning your focus is concentrated accordingly.

### Tactics to sharpen the pen

**1. Time-box your sessions.**

Writing is actually quite pleasant and sustainable once you enter a flow state. Pressure often draws out focus, which melts into flow after 5 or so minutes. [This app](https://www.squibler.io/dangerous-writing-prompt-app) operationalizes said pressure by erasing what you've written if you don't keep adding to it at a steady pace. Alternatively, try to accomplish writing goals (e.g. finish a post) in shorter-than-usual durations of time to invoke [Parkinson's Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkinson's_Law) in a productive direction.

**2. Schedule writing time.**

This one's obvious. When you don't give a block of time a certain goal, you become more likely to engage in low-activation-energy activities that you don't actually endorse but aren't lucid enough to snap out of (e.g. doomscrolling). So, schedule blocks of time dedicated to things you think are important!

Tentatively, I'm scheduling non-work writing blocks on Tuesday and Thursday evenings. My hope is my head will be abuzz with interesting concepts I've encountered at work. Otherwise, I'll write on weekends about whatever I want to learn more about. Recently, I've been cataloguing my world model as it pertains to AI risk.

**3. Vary your verbiage through elimination.**

Constraints breed creativity! Try to make a point of using a specific turn of phrase or non-standard descriptor word only once in a piece. Repeated elements signal low creativity, and generating replacement expressions is rewarding and an easy way to improve your writing quality.

**4. Separate journaling from writing.**

I should write more. I should also journal more. These are separate activities, and should stay as such. Journaling chiefly involves self-reflection, where I talk about how my life is going and how I feel about it. Of course, journaling can touch on concepts I tackle in my writing, but entries should revolve around my life and events therein.

On the other hand, writing is the practice of articulating, or "pointing at", some concept of how things are in the world. You generally write to understand the world better, or to output your thoughts on a part of it. Failing to delineate which of the two I'm doing often turns personal reflections into half-baked tangents whose ideas are only tenuously linked to my intended subject.

**5. Turn exploratory writing into directed writing.**

Put simply, give all writing sessions a legible (and hopefully verifiable) goal. I often write under the pretense that I'll publish my output on my blog. Potential public scrutiny incentivizes me to strive for sound reasoning, tasteful word selection, sensible structure, concise length, and useful conclusions for myself and other readers. I'm not sure this should generally be applied, as prettifying a post for public dissemination involves a lot of activities that are distinctly not refining world models or untangling reasoning in pursuit of truth.

**6. Don't write about just anything.**

Nowadays, I want to write about every passing idea I find superficially interesting. Unfortunately, you need some discernment because many thoughts aren't substantive enough to warrant a high-fidelity exploration, and marginal energy is generally better spent elsewhere. For me, sharpening my writing focus looks like deprioritizing academic subjects that aren't relevant to my career, as those are 1) far enough out of my wheelhouse that a resulting exploration would be blindingly ignorant without non-trivial research time and 2) not instrumentally useful for my goals (which I'd rather focus marginal time on).

I also have an urge to blacklist other subjects (e.g. hard-to-articulate interpersonal feelings or hard science topics that already attract lots of discussion), but every other type of writing seems worth devoting energy to. For me, this is because my marginal contributions may contain novel value or at least will improve my understanding in ways instrumentally useful for my goals.
